\chapter{LITERATURE REVIEW}

The integration of artificial intelligence into talent acquisition has generated a vast body of academic literature and industrial whitepapers. Recent studies from 2024 to 2026 reflect a distinct shift away from rudimentary parsing toward semantic understanding, bias mitigation, and algorithm transparency. This chapter surveys the current landscape across multiple operational themes to contextualize the proposed prototype.

\section{Traditional Keyword-Based Systems}
Early iterations of resume parsing and ATS platforms were heavily reliant on rule-based engines and simple Boolean search logic. As established by recent comparative reviews of industry practices, these systems extract text and simply count the frequency of target words. While computationally negligible and exceptionally easy to deploy, such conventional ATS systems suffer from unacceptably high false-negative rates. Candidates utilizing synonyms, alternative phrasing, or descriptive prose, such as ``orchestrated a financial turnaround'' instead of the explicit keyword ``leadership'', are routinely discarded. Furthermore, these systems exhibit almost no resistance to adversarial manipulation; applicants can effortlessly artificially inflate their scores through keyword dilution or hidden text. The literature overwhelmingly concludes that pure keyword counting is obsolete for modern talent acquisition.

\section{Machine Learning and TF-IDF Models}
To improve upon rigid boolean logic without incurring the massive financial and computational overhead of deep learning, researchers have increasingly leveraged traditional Machine Learning (ML) alongside statistical Natural Language Processing (NLP). Tools utilizing libraries such as SpaCy for Named Entity Recognition (NER) combined with Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) have proven highly effective for initial feature extraction and structured parsing. TF-IDF mathematically evaluates how important a specific word is to a document relative to a larger corpus, inherently penalizing overly common terms and naturally mitigating certain forms of naive keyword stuffing. When coupled with mathematical distance algorithms like Cosine Similarity, TF-IDF vectors can generate relevance scores between a job description and a candidate's resume that far exceed the accuracy of simple keyword counts, while still executing in mere milliseconds on standard CPU hardware.

\section{Transformer and Semantic Matching Models}
The most significant technological leap in automated resume screening over the past three years has been the widespread adoption of Transformer-based architectures. Studies involving BERT and its optimized variants, such as Sentence-BERT, demonstrate an unparalleled ability to contextualize human language. Deshmukh and Raut (2025) illustrated that BERT-based NLP models can significantly improve screening efficiency by recognizing that terms like ``Python'' and ``Django'' belong to the same semantic cluster, effectively solving the keyword mismatch problem. Similarly, the Resume2Vec framework (2025) showcased a Transformer-based pipeline that outperformed traditional ATS systems by mapping candidate skills into dense multidimensional vector spaces, achieving massive improvements in Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (nDCG) metrics. More recently, multi-agent LLM frameworks, such as the one proposed by Lo et al. (2025), utilize Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) to dynamically evaluate resumes across diverse hiring criteria, achieving screening accuracy closely comparable to senior human HR professionals.

\section{Explainable AI (XAI) in Recruitment}
As AI assumes a much larger and more powerful role in gatekeeping employment opportunities, the opacity of complex algorithms has become a critical legal and ethical liability. The ``black box'' nature of deep neural networks prevents recruiters from fundamentally understanding why a candidate received a 45\% versus an 85\% match score. Explainable AI (XAI) seeks to make these complex models transparent. Recent literature has focused heavily on post-hoc explanation methods like SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) and LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations). These mathematical frameworks attempt to reverse-engineer model predictions to show exactly which features, such as years of experience versus a specific technical skill, contributed most heavily to the final classification score. However, while mathematically rigorous, SHAP and LIME are computationally heavy and produce visualizations that require significant data science expertise to interpret correctly. There remains a distinct research gap for simpler, rule-based XAI outputs that generate plain-English rationales directly tailored for HR personnel without requiring a statistical background.

\section{Bias, Fairness, and Format Discrimination}
Algorithmic bias in automated recruitment is a heavily scrutinized academic domain. While demographic bias, such as penalizing candidates based on inferred gender, race, or age, is widely recognized and targeted for mitigation, ``Format Bias'' or ``Layout Bias'' is a subtle but equally destructive issue. Automated digital parsers often fail catastrophically when encountering non-standard, multi-column, or highly graphical resumes. Candidates using complex PDF layouts to stand out visually may actually cause the parser to scramble their text streams, resulting in an artificially low score due to unrecognized boundaries. Recent document layout analysis research highlights the absolute necessity of robust Optical Character Recognition (OCR) fallback mechanisms, such as Tesseract, and hierarchical spacing recognition to ensure that a candidate's format choice does not inadvertently hinder their algorithmic evaluation.

\section{Cost, Scalability, and Deployment Constraints}
Despite the overwhelming academic success of LLMs and Sentence-BERT models in solving semantic mismatches, their real-world deployment is severely hindered by cost and computational requirements. Utilizing 7-billion or 70-billion parameter models to process thousands of resumes simultaneously requires vast amounts of memory and expensive cloud GPU instances, locking out SMEs from accessing top-tier AI capabilities. Consequently, there is a distinct and growing market demand for lightweight, low-cost screening platforms that do not require enterprise-grade infrastructure. These tools intentionally prioritize rapid bulk processing, deterministic behavior, and explainability over perfect semantic understanding, serving highly effectively as initial ``first-pass'' filters.

\section{Identified Research Gaps}
The synthesis of the current literature reveals a highly polarized technological landscape. On one extreme exist traditional keyword matchers that are fast and cheap but highly inaccurate and easily manipulated. On the other extreme exist semantic LLMs that are highly accurate but prohibitively expensive, slow, and opaque. The primary research gap lies squarely in the middle: the development of a structured, explainable, and highly resource-efficient methodology. This methodology must improve upon raw keyword matching through TF-IDF and NLP heuristics, enforce strict mathematical boundaries against adversarial cheating, standardize document inputs via OCR to prevent layout bias, and generate ``white box'' plain-English explanations, all while executing in milliseconds on standard CPU hardware.
